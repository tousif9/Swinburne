Plant Genera

Named After People

Many genus names honour scientists. Beilschmiedia is named after the German botanist Carl Traugott Beilschmied. Other names describe the plant instead: Dipterocarpus means "two-winged fruit" and Cryptocarya means "hidden nut".

About This Page

A genus (plural: genera) is a smaller group of closely related species inside a family. The genus name is always written first in a scientific name, in italics and with a capital letter. Here are two genera from each of our featured families.

On this page

  • Dipterocarpus
  • Monotes
  • Beilschmiedia
  • Cryptocarya

Four Questions to Identify the Genus

  1. Which family does the plant belong to?
  2. Does the fruit have wings? If so, count them: two long wings suggests Dipterocarpus, five equal wings suggests Monotes.
  3. If the fruit is fleshy, is it hidden by the tepals when young? That suggests Cryptocarya.
  4. Is the fruit a large green globe or a waxy dark-purple plum? That suggests Beilschmiedia.

Dipterocarpus

Keruing

The dipterocarp family takes its name from Dipterocarpus, a genus whose name means "two-winged fruit" in Greek. With approximately 70 species, it is the third-largest genus in the family. It is found throughout South and Southeast Asia, from Sri Lanka and India to the Philippines, and is an important part of dipterocarp forests. Borneo is the most diverse, with many species found only there, such as Dipterocarpus sarawakensis, the "Sarawak keruing".

These trees are used for keruing timber, plywood and a resinous oil (gurjun, kanyin or keruing oil), and a few of them are used in traditional herbal medicine. The oldest known dipterocarp fossil also comes from this genus, dating from the end of the Cretaceous period in India.

Quick facts

Family
Dipterocarpaceae
Common name
Keruing (timber), gurjun (oil)
Size
About 70 species, 65 of them currently accepted
Origin and range
South and Southeast Asia, from Sri Lanka and India to the Philippines, mainly concentrated in Borneo
Name meaning
Greek for "two-winged fruit"
Species on this site
D. alatus and D. tuberculatus

How to recognise it

  • Fruit with two long wings
  • Trees with tall trunks that can be tapped for resinous oil
  • Occurs throughout South and Southeast Asia, with the most species in Borneo

Monotes

No common name

Monotes is a genus of flowering plants in the Dipterocarpaceae family. The name means "unity" or "uniqueness", and it was given when this was the only dipterocarp genus recorded from Africa. There are 23 accepted species. Their diversity is highest in the Zambezian region of south-central Africa, and one species, Monotes madagascariensis, reaches Madagascar.

Quick facts

Family
Dipterocarpaceae
Size
23 accepted species
Origin and range
Tropical Africa, centred on the Zambezian region, and Madagascar
Name meaning
"Unity" or "uniqueness", as it was once the only African dipterocarp genus
Species on this site
M. adenophyllus and M. glaber

How to recognise it

  • An African dipterocarp, found mainly in the Zambezian region
  • Only one species, Monotes madagascariensis, is known to grow in Madagascar

Beilschmiedia

No common name for the whole genus

Beilschmiedia is a huge genus of trees and shrubs in the laurel family, with 267 accepted species. Most are tropical and a few are temperate; the genus occurs throughout tropical Asia, Africa, Madagascar, Australia, New Zealand, and North, Central and South America, including the Caribbean. It is adapted to a wider variety of climates than most other laurel genera. Many species grow in cloud forest and tropical rainforest, with the highest diversity in Asia and Southeast Asia, while others live in drier Mediterranean-type climates. Some, like the Chilean bellotos B. berteroana and B. miersii, are even frost hardy and popular with gardeners in colder climates.

The leaves are leathery and dark green with sunken veins, the young leaves are reddish, and the branchlets are covered in fine reddish-brown hairs. The bark is smooth or rough and brown. The small flowers are greenish to cream and grow in upright clusters. The fruits are one-seeded, ranging from very large green globes to dark purple, plum-like fruits with a waxy bloom, and many are eaten by birds that spread the seeds. In New Zealand, the big seeds of the taraire are too large for any bird except the native pigeon to swallow and disperse. Some species provide valuable timber, and the seeds of B. bancroftii were eaten by Aboriginal Australians.

Quick facts

Family
Lauraceae
Common names
None for the genus; taraire and tawa (New Zealand) and belloto (Chile) are names of single species
Size
267 accepted species
Origin and range
Tropical Asia, Africa, Madagascar, Australia, New Zealand, North, Central and South America and the Caribbean
Type species
B. roxburghiana (Himalayas to southern China, Indochina and Peninsular Malaysia)
Uses
Valuable timber; seeds of B. bancroftii eaten by Aboriginal Australians; frost-hardy species grown in gardens
Species on this site
B. berteroana and B. costaricensis

How to recognise it

  • Dark green, leathery leaves with sunken veins, opposite or alternate depending on the species
  • Reddish young leaves and branchlets with fine reddish-brown hairs
  • Small greenish to cream flowers in upright clusters at the base of the leaves
  • One-seeded fruits, from large green globes to waxy dark-purple plums

Cryptocarya

No common name

About 360 species of Cryptocarya belong to the laurel family. They are mostly trees, with a few shrubs, and grow in parts of South America, southern Africa, Madagascar, South and Southeast Asia, Japan, New Guinea and Australia. The genus was first described by the botanist Robert Brown in 1810, and its name, "hidden nut", refers to the fruit being enclosed by the surrounding parts of the flower. DNA studies show that it is closely related to Beilschmiedia, Potameia and Endiandra.

The leaves are usually arranged singly along the branches on short stalks, with veins branching from a central midrib. The small flowers grow in clusters towards the ends of the branches; each has 6 tepals and 9 stamens in two rows, with both male and female parts. The fruit is a fleshy, oval to round drupe with one seed. Some species are useful to people: ravensara oil, from C. agathophylla of eastern Madagascar, is exported to Europe and America for aromatherapy, and leaves of C. woodii found at prehistoric African settlements were probably used to keep insects away.

Quick facts

Family
Lauraceae
Size
About 360 species
Origin and range
South America, southern Africa, Madagascar, South and Southeast Asia, Japan, New Guinea and Australia
Name meaning
"Hidden nut", as the fruit is hidden by the tepals
First described
Robert Brown, 1810
Closest relatives
Beilschmiedia, Potameia and Endiandra
Uses
Ravensara oil for aromatherapy (C. agathophylla); insect control in the past (C. woodii)
Species on this site
C. adpressa and C. alba

How to recognise it

  • Leaves usually alternate and stalked, with veins branching from a central midrib
  • Small flowers with 6 tepals and 9 stamens in 2 rows, clustered near the branch tips
  • Fleshy, oval to round one-seeded fruit, hidden by the tepals when young
