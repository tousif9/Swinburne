Plant Families

Family Names

Plant family names nearly always end in -aceae. The first part usually comes from one well-known genus in the family. Dipterocarpaceae is named after Dipterocarpus, and Lauraceae after Laurus, the bay laurel you may use in your cooking.

About This Page

A plant family is a large group of related plants that have important features in common, such as the way their flowers and fruit are built. On this page you'll meet the two families we feature on Pl@netMatrix. Follow the links in each section to explore their genera and species.

On this page

  • Dipterocarpaceae

How to Tell the Two Families Apart

  1. Look at the fruit. Dipterocarps have winged fruits, while laurels have fleshy berries.
  2. Gently crush a leaf. Laurel leaves usually smell spicy or citrusy.
  3. Check the bark for sticky, sweet-smelling resin, a sure sign of a dipterocarp.
  4. Look closely at the leaves. Many laurels have glossy, waxy leaves with a pointed "drip tip".

Dipterocarpaceae

The dipterocarp family

Dipterocarpaceae is a family of flowering plants with about 22 genera and nearly 700 species, most of them trees of the lowland tropical forest. You'll find them right across the tropics, from northern South America and Africa to India, Southeast Asia and the Philippines. Borneo, though, has the greatest variety, and there dipterocarps have been the backbone of the lowland rainforest for millions of years. Many are giants that rise above the rest of the forest, growing 40 to 70 metres tall. The tallest one known is a yellow meranti in Danum Valley, Sabah. Nicknamed Menara, Malay for "tower", it stands an amazing 93 metres high.

The name comes from Greek words meaning "two-winged fruit". Sadly, nearly every species in Borneo is now at risk of dying out. Dipterocarps supply much of the region's wood, plywood, resin and fragrant oils, and too many trees are being cut, logged illegally or cleared along with the forest.

Quick facts

Scientific name
Dipterocarpaceae
Size
22 genera, about 695 species
Largest genera
Shorea (196 species), Hopea (104), Dipterocarpus (70) and Vatica (65)
Origin and range
Throughout the tropics, especially in Borneo
Subfamilies
Dipterocarpoideae (mainly Asia) and Monotoideae (Africa, Madagascar and the Colombian Amazon)
Genera on this site
Dipterocarpus and Monotes
Species on this site
D. alatus, D. tuberculatus, M. adenophyllus and M. glaber

How to recognise it

  • Fruits with papery wings: two in Dipterocarpus, more in some other genera
  • Tall, straight trunks, often rising above the rest of the forest
  • Fragrant, resinous wood
  • Can be evergreen or lose their leaves, and grow anywhere from sea level up to about 1,300 metres

Lauraceae

The laurel family

The true bay laurel and its close relatives make up the laurel family, Lauraceae. It's a big family, with about 2,850 to 3,500 species in more than 50 genera. They grow in warm and tropical places all over the world, especially Southeast Asia and South America. Most are evergreen, fragrant trees and shrubs. A few, like Sassafras, drop their leaves each year, and one genus, Cassytha, is made up entirely of vines that live off other plants. Laurels are an important part of tropical forests, from the lowlands up into the mountains, and they lend their name to the glossy-leaved "laurel forests" and cloud forests on both sides of the equator. The family is also very old: fossil flowers date back to the middle of the Cretaceous period, 90 to 98 million years ago.

Many laurels are full of natural oils that stop animals from eating them. People have found plenty of uses for them: cinnamon and cassia, camphor, bay leaves and avocados all come from this family, and laurel wood is used for timber and for chests that keep insects away. The fruit is a one-seeded berry, sometimes sitting in a cup like an acorn. Birds, monkeys and other animals love to eat it, and they spread the seeds as they go. Unfortunately, overcutting, illegal logging and the loss of their forest homes have put many species in danger of disappearing.

Quick facts

Scientific name
Lauraceae
Size
58 accepted genera and an estimated 2,850 to 3,500 species (estimates vary)
Origin and range
Warm temperate and tropical regions throughout the world, particularly Southeast Asia and South America
Popular members of the family
Cinnamon and cassia (Cinnamomum), camphor (Camphora), bay laurel (Laurus) and avocado (Persea)
Subfamilies
Lauroideae and Cassythoideae (Cassytha only)
Age
Fossils from the mid-Cretaceous, 90 to 98 million years old
Genera on this site
Beilschmiedia and Cryptocarya
Species on this site
B. berteroana, B. costaricensis, C. adpressa and C. alba

How to recognise it

  • Fragrant leaves, bark and wood, rich in natural oils
  • Pointed oval leaves, many of them glossy and waxy, with a "drip tip" that lets rain run off
  • One-seeded fruits that may sit in a small cup, like an acorn
  • Flowers act as female first and male later (called protogynous), which helps prevent inbreeding
