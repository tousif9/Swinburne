Plant Families

Family Names

Plant family names almost always end in -aceae. The first part comes from one important genus in the family: Dipterocarpaceae is named after Dipterocarpus, and Lauraceae after Laurus, the bay laurel used in cooking.

About This Page

A plant family is a large group of related plants that share key features, such as the structure of their flowers and fruit. This page introduces the two families featured on Pl@netMatrix. Follow the links in each section to explore their genera and species.

On this page

  • Dipterocarpaceae
  • Lauraceae

How to Tell the Two Families Apart

  1. Look at the fruit: dipterocarps have winged fruits, laurels have fleshy berries.
  2. Crush a leaf gently: laurel leaves usually smell spicy or citrusy.
  3. Check the bark for sticky, fragrant resin, which is typical of dipterocarps.
  4. Look at the leaves: many laurels have glossy, waxy leaves with a pointed "drip tip".

Dipterocarpaceae

The dipterocarp family

Dipterocarpaceae is a family of flowering plants made up of about 22 genera and nearly 700 species, most of them trees of lowland tropical forest. The family occurs throughout the tropics, ranging from northern South America and Africa to India, Southeast Asia and the Philippines, but is most richly developed in Borneo, where dipterocarps have been the key elements of the lowland rainforest for millions of years. Many are large emergent trees that grow to between 40 and 70 metres tall. A yellow meranti tree in Danum Valley, Sabah, known as Menara ("tower" in Malay), is the tallest known, measuring 93 metres.

The name comes from Greek words for "two-winged fruit". Nearly all species in Borneo are at risk of extinction, as the dipterocarps provide a large proportion of the wood, plywood, resin and aromatic oils of the region, which are all being overcut, illegally harvested and felled through forest clearance.

Source: Wikipedia

Dipterocarpaceae

Shorea (196 species), Hopea (104), Dipterocarpus (70) and Vatica (65)
Origin and range
Throughout the tropics, especially in Borneo
Subfamilies
Dipterocarpoideae (mainly Asia) and Monotoideae (Africa, Madagascar and the Colombian Amazon)
Genera on this site
Dipterocarpus and Monotes
Species on this site
D. alatus, D. tuberculatus, M. adenophyllus and M. glaber

How to recognise it

  • Fruits with papery wings: two in Dipterocarpus, more in some other genera
  • Tall, straight trunks, often rising above the rest of the forest
  • Fragrant, resinous wood
  • Can be evergreen or deciduous, growing from sea level up to about 1,300 metres

Lauraceae

The laurel family

The true bay laurel and its close relatives belong to the laurel family, Lauraceae. It is a large family of about 2,850 to 3,500 species in over 50 genera, found in warm temperate and tropical areas throughout the world, particularly in Southeast Asia and South America. The vast majority are evergreen, aromatic and woody; a few, like Sassafras, are deciduous, and the genus Cassytha consists entirely of parasitic vines. Laurels are an important part of lowland and montane tropical forests, and they give their name to the glossy-leaved "laurel forests" and cloud forests of both hemispheres. The family is very old, with fossil flowers from the middle of the Cretaceous period (90 to 98 million years ago).

Many laurel trees contain essential oils that keep animals from eating the plants. People use them in many ways, including cinnamon and cassia, camphor, bay leaves and avocados, as well as laurel wood for timber and insect-repelling chests. The fruit is a one-seeded, berry-like drupe, sometimes set in a cup like an acorn, and it is eaten by many birds, monkeys and other animals that spread the seeds. Unfortunately, many species are in danger of extinction because of overcutting, illegal logging and habitat destruction.

Source: Wikipedia

Quick facts

Scientific name
Lauraceae
Size
58 accepted genera and an estimated 2,850 to 3,500 species (estimates vary)
Origin and range
Warm temperate and tropical regions throughout the world, particularly Southeast Asia and South America
Popular members of the family
Cinnamon and cassia (Cinnamomum), camphor (Camphora), bay laurel (Laurus) and avocado (Persea)
Subfamilies
Lauroideae and Cassythoideae (Cassytha only)
Age
Fossils from the mid-Cretaceous, 90 to 98 million years old
Genera on this site
Beilschmiedia and Cryptocarya
Species on this site
B. berteroana, B. costaricensis, C. adpressa and C. alba

How to recognise it

  • Aromatic leaves, bark and wood with a high essential oil content
  • Pointed-oval leaves, many glossy and waxy with a "drip tip" that sheds rain
  • One-seeded fruits that may sit in a small cup like an acorn
  • Flowers go through a female stage first (protogynous), which reduces inbreeding
