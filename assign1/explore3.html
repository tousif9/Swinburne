Plant Species

Protecting Species

Scientists record how threatened each species is on the IUCN Red List. Several species on this page are losing their habitat to logging and farming. Every photo shared through our contribution form helps show where these plants still grow.

About This Page

A species is a single kind of plant. Its scientific name has two parts: the genus, followed by a species name that often describes the plant or where it grows. This page profiles two species from each of our four genera, with photos, origin and key features.

How to Identify a Species in the Field

  1. Work out the family and genus first using the Families and Genera pages.
  2. Note the size and shape of the leaves, and whether they are hairy.
  3. Look for flowers or fruit, which are the most reliable clues.
  4. Record where you found it, since many species only grow in one region.
  5. Upload your photo on the Identify page to check your answer.

Dipterocarpus alatus

Resin tree

Dipterocarpus alatus, the resin tree, is a large tropical tree that grows 30 to 60 metres tall. It is found in dense evergreen and semi-evergreen lowland forests and along riverbanks in tropical Asia, sometimes in clusters. It needs moist, well-drained soil and tolerates flooding, but not strong winds or fire. Its flowers are pollinated by insects, its fruit ripens from March to April, and the winged seeds are carried by the wind.

People have long used this tree. Its wood is used for construction and furniture, and its oily resin is tapped for lacquer, traditional medicine and sealing boats against leaks; mixed with beeswax, the resin is also used to dress ulcerated wounds. The bark of young trees is used as a remedy for liver disease and rheumatism. Today it is listed as Vulnerable, and it is one of the main trees planted to restore forest around the Dong Nai River and Cat Tien National Park in Vietnam.

Source: Wikipedia

Dipterocarpus tuberculatus

Gurjun tree (Khmer: khlông)

Dipterocarpus tuberculatus, known as the gurjun tree in Indian English or khlông in Khmer, is a dipterocarp from mainland Southeast Asia. It grows naturally in Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos and Vietnam, in open lowland forests up to about 800 to 1,000 metres above sea level. It reaches a height of 5 to 25 metres.

Many parts of the tree are used. Its oils and wood are used in the Indian subcontinent. In Cambodia its leaves are used to wrap goods and sometimes to cover huts, its weather-resistant wood is made into beams, boards and boats, and its roots are used in traditional medicine to heal broken bones. It is also an important source of firewood in some areas of Kampong Chhnang province.

Source: Wikipedia

Monotes adenophyllus

No common name

Monotes adenophyllus is a shrub or small tree in the dipterocarp family, native to south-central Africa. It is found in western and southwestern Tanzania, northern Zambia, the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo and northern Angola. It typically grows 4 to 8 metres tall and can sometimes reach 12 metres.

It grows in miombo woodland and in bushy or wooded savanna, often on shallow, rocky or iron-rich soils known as lateritic soils, between 840 and 1,500 metres above sea level. Botanists recognise three forms of this species: var. adenophyllus, which covers the whole range; var. homblei, found in Tanzania, northern Zambia, the southern DR Congo and northern Angola; and f. albidus, known only from the southern DR Congo.

Source: Wikipedia

Monotes glaber

No common name

Monotes glaber is a tree in the dipterocarp family from south-central Africa. It is found from the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo through Angola, Zambia and Zimbabwe to northern Botswana. It is a small tree, 3 to 10 metres tall, and is common and widespread in miombo woodland on sandy soil. The botanist Thomas Archibald Sprague first described it in 1909. The name glaber comes from Latin and means "smooth" or "hairless".

Source: Wikipedia

Beilschmiedia berteroana

Southern acorn tree (Spanish: belloto del sur)

Beilschmiedia berteroana, the southern acorn tree or belloto del sur, is a threatened evergreen tree that lives only in Chile, between about 35 and 37 degrees south. It can reach 30 metres in height, with a trunk up to one metre wide and grey bark with long vertical cracks. Its glossy green, oval leaves are 3 to 7.5 cm long, grow in pairs or nearly in pairs, and are bluish-grey underneath. In July and August it produces clusters of greenish-yellow flowers, followed by a green fruit 1.5 to 2 cm across that contains a single seed and has a small point at the tip.

The southern acorn tree can grow up to 1,800 metres above sea level and tolerates frost better than most of its tropical relatives. One place it can be seen is La Campana National Park in Chile, where it grows alongside the endangered Chilean wine palm. Its sweet fruit is eaten locally, the bark was used to tan leather, and its hard, attractive wood is prized. Bees make honey from its flowers, and it is planted as an ornamental tree in Chile and, rarely, in Spain.

Source: Wikipedia

Beilschmiedia costaricensis

No common name

Beilschmiedia costaricensis is an evergreen tree in the laurel family, and its species name comes from Costa Rica. It grows in the misty highland forests of Central America, which have humid air, rain every month and cooler temperatures than the lowlands. It usually grows into a medium to large tree and forms part of the upper layer of the forest.

Its leaves are simple and placed alternately on the stem. They are thick and leathery, which helps reduce water loss. The flowers are small and plain, and insects mainly carry pollen from one flower to another. Each fruit has one seed, and birds and mammals eat the fruit and carry the seed elsewhere, which allows new trees to start. The bark is smooth when the tree is young and becomes rough and cracked as it ages. Little has been written about how Beilschmiedia costaricensis is used, and the tree is rarely planted outside its forests.

Source: iNaturalist

Cryptocarya adpressa

No common name (described in 2016)

Cryptocarya adpressa is an evergreen tree in the laurel family. It is found only on New Caledonia, an island group in the south-west Pacific, and was officially described for the first time in 2016 by the botanists Jérôme Munzinger and Gordon McPherson. It grows in dense, humid tropical forest and can reach six to twenty-five metres tall. Its trunk is fifteen to twenty centimetres across, with brown bark that ranges from slightly rough to nearly smooth.

Its leaves are small, with veins that branch out like a feather from a central rib. One key feature that sets it apart from relatives such as Cryptocarya leptospermoides and Cryptocarya gracilis is the layer of fine hairs on its flower clusters. These hairs stay in place and lie flat against the surface, which is where the name comes from: adpressa means "pressed close" in Latin, referring to the flat-lying hairs.

Source: iNaturalist

Cryptocarya alba

Peumo, Chilean acorn

Cryptocarya alba, also called peumo or the Chilean acorn, is an evergreen tree that grows in central Chile. It can live in both wet and dry places, reaches up to 1,500 metres above sea level, and grows alongside the endangered Chilean wine palm. It can grow up to 20 metres tall, with a straight trunk up to one metre wide and greyish bark. Thick branches rise from the centre of the tree, and thin twigs hang from their ends.

Its egg-shaped leaves are 2.5 to 8.5 centimetres long, slightly wavy and strongly scented. Small greenish-yellow flowers appear in clusters from November to January, followed by edible red berries that are also called peumos. Each fruit has a heavy seed that sprouts easily. The hard wood resists moisture, and the bark is used to tan leather and to make an orange dye. Peumo is also grown as an ornamental and fruit tree, and it has done well when planted in northern California and in Spain.

Source: Wikipedia
