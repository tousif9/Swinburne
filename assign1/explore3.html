Plant Species

Protecting Species

Scientists record how threatened each species is on the IUCN Red List. Several species on this page are losing their habitat to logging and farming. Every photo shared through our contribution form helps show where these plants still grow.

About This Page

A species is a single kind of plant. Its scientific name has two parts: the genus, followed by a species name that often describes the plant or where it grows. This page profiles two species from each of our four genera, with photos, origin and key features.

How to Identify a Species in the Field

  1. Work out the family and genus first using the Families and Genera pages.
  2. Note the size and shape of the leaves, and whether they are hairy.
  3. Look for flowers or fruit, which are the most reliable clues.
  4. Record where you found it, since many species only grow in one region.
  5. Upload your photo on the Identify page to check your answer.

Dipterocarpus alatus

Yang na

A tall, straight-trunked tree that often grows near rivers in lowland forest. Its smooth grey trunk is famous for its oily resin, which villagers tap by cutting a pocket in the bark. The resin has been used for torches, varnish and waterproofing boats. The species name alatus means "winged", describing its winged fruit.

Quick facts

Classification
Dipterocarpaceae › Dipterocarpus
Common name
Yang na, hairy-leaf apitong
Origin
Lowland forests of mainland Southeast Asia, from Myanmar and Thailand to Cambodia, Laos and Vietnam
Height
Up to about 40 metres
Conservation
Threatened by logging and forest clearing

How to recognise it

  • Oval leaves, softly hairy underneath
  • Resin pocket cut into the lower trunk
  • Fruit with two long wings

Dipterocarpus tuberculatus

In tree

This species is easy to recognise by its enormous, stiff leaves, which can be larger than a dinner plate. Local people use them for thatching roofs and wrapping food. Unlike most dipterocarps, it grows in open, dry forest and drops its leaves in the dry season. Its thick bark protects it from the ground fires that sweep through these forests.

Quick facts

Classification
Dipterocarpaceae › Dipterocarpus
Common name
In tree (Myanmar), phluang (Thailand)
Origin
Dry deciduous forests of mainland Southeast Asia, including Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam
Height
Usually 20 to 30 metres
Habitat
Open, seasonally dry forest that burns in the dry season

How to recognise it

  • Very large, broad leaves
  • Leaves turn orange and fall in the dry season
  • Thick, fire-resistant bark

Monotes adenophyllus

Gland-leaf monotes

A small, deciduous tree of Africa's dry woodlands. Its name refers to a small gland on the underside of the leaf near the stalk. The rounded leaves have a wrinkled, net-veined surface and are often hairy underneath, which helps reduce water loss during the long dry season.

Quick facts

Classification
Dipterocarpaceae › Monotes
Meaning of the name
adenophyllus means "gland leaf"
Origin
Woodlands of southern tropical Africa, including Zambia, Zimbabwe and Mozambique
Height
A shrub or small tree, usually under 10 metres
Habitat
Dry miombo woodland on sandy soils

How to recognise it

  • Rounded, wrinkled leaves with a notch at the base
  • Pale-yellow flowers with fluffy stamens
  • Pale, blocky bark

Monotes glaber

Smooth-leaved monotes

As its name suggests, this species has smooth, hairless leaves, which separates it from hairier relatives such as Monotes adenophyllus. It grows in open, grassy woodland where trees stand well apart. Before falling in the dry season, its leaves turn yellow, orange and red.

Quick facts

Classification
Dipterocarpaceae › Monotes
Meaning of the name
glaber means "smooth" or "hairless"
Origin
Tropical Africa, including Zambia, Zimbabwe, Malawi, Tanzania and Angola
Height
A small tree up to about 12 metres
Habitat
Open miombo woodland and wooded grassland

How to recognise it

  • Smooth, hairless oval leaves
  • Colourful leaves before the dry season
  • Grows in open, grassy woodland

Beilschmiedia berteroana

Belloto del sur

This rare evergreen tree grows only in a small area of Chile, mostly along streams and in shady ravines. Much of its habitat has been cleared for farms and plantations, so it is now endangered. Conservation groups grow seedlings in nurseries and replant them to help the species recover.

Quick facts

Classification

Conservation

  • Smooth, pale bark
  • Glossy oval leaves with pale undersides
  • Tiny yellow-green flowers in branched clusters

Beilschmiedia costaricensis

Aguacatillo

A rainforest tree of Central America whose small, olive-shaped fruits look like tiny avocados, which is how it got its local name. The fruits ripen from green to glossy black and are eaten by forest birds and mammals. Its leaves grow in opposite pairs and are often covered in moss in the damp forest.

Quick facts

Classification
Lauraceae › Beilschmiedia
Common name
Aguacatillo ("little avocado")
Origin
Humid forests of Central America, including Costa Rica and Panama
Height
A medium to large rainforest tree
Habitat
Wet lowland and mountain rainforest

How to recognise it

  • Leaves in opposite pairs
  • Long, branching clusters of tiny flower buds
  • Olive-shaped fruits that ripen black

Cryptocarya adpressa

No widely used common name

A shrub or small tree of cool, misty forest. Its new leaves are a bright yellow-green that stands out against the darker older leaves. The flowers are tiny and pale yellow, and they are followed by round, blotchy green fruits. Botanists sometimes make a small cut in the bark to check the colour and smell of the wood, which helps identify laurels.

Quick facts

Classification
Lauraceae › Cryptocarya
Meaning of the name
adpressa means "pressed close"
Origin
Humid, often misty mountain forest
Height
A shrub or small tree
Habitat
Understorey of cool, wet forest

How to recognise it

  • Bright yellow-green new leaves
  • Round, blotchy green fruits
  • Orange wood under the bark

Cryptocarya alba

Peumo

Peumo is an evergreen tree from central Chile with aromatic leaves that are whitish underneath, which is where the name alba ("white") comes from. It produces bright pink-red fruits that people in Chile have traditionally eaten after cooking. The tree survives hot, dry summers and is an important part of Chile's native forest.

Quick facts

Habitat

  • Bright pink-red fruits
  • Dense, rounded evergreen crown
