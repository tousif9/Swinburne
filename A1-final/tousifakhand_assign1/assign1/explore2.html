Plant Genera

Named After People

Many genus names come from scientists. For instance, Beilschmiedia is named for Carl Traugott Beilschmied, a German botanist. Others just describe the plant: Dipterocarpus means "two-winged fruit" and Cryptocarya "hidden nut".

About This Page

A genus is a smaller group of closely related organisms within a family (more than one are called genera). The first part of a scientific name is always its name and is in italics with a capital letter. Below you'll find two genera from each of our two families.

On this page

  • Dipterocarpus
  • Monotes
  • Beilschmiedia
  • Cryptocarya

Four Questions to Identify the Genus

  1. What is the plant's family?
  2. Are there wings on the fruit? If so, count them. Two long wings lead to Dipterocarpus, five equal wings lead to Monotes.
  3. If the fruit is fleshy, is it embedded in the petal-like parts of the flower in the early stages? If so, it is most likely Cryptocarya.
  4. The fruit: is it a big green ball or a dark purple, waxy plum? If so it is probably Beilschmiedia.

Dipterocarpus

Keruing

The whole dipterocarp family is named for Dipterocarpus, Greek for "two-winged fruit". With about 70 species, it's the third-largest genus in the family. It is widely distributed throughout South and South-East Asia, ranging from Sri Lanka and India to the Philippines, and is an important component of dipterocarp forests. Borneo is the most varied with a number of species found nowhere else, including the "Sarawak keruing", Dipterocarpus sarawakensis.

Keruing trees provide keruing timber, plywood and a resin-rich oil called gurjun, kanyin or keruing oil, and a few are also utilized as traditional herbal medicines. The oldest fossil of dipterocarp belongs to this genus as well. It's of Indian origin and dates from the very late Cretaceous.

Source: Wikipedia

D. alatus and D. tuberculatus

How to recognise it

  • Fruit with two long wings
  • Trees with tall trunks, from which a resinous oil can be extracted.
  • Found all over South and Southeast Asia, with the majority of the species in Borneo

Monotes

No common name

Monotes is a genus of flowering plants of the family Dipterocarpaceae. Its name means "unity" or "uniqueness" because at the time of its naming it was the only genus of dipterocarp in Africa. Twenty-three species are now accepted. They are mostly of the Zambezian zone of south-central Africa, with one, Monotes madagascariensis, extending to Madagascar.

Source: Wikipedia

Quick facts

Family
Dipterocarpaceae
Size
23 accepted species
Origin and range
Tropical Africa, centred on the Zambezian region, and Madagascar
Name meaning

M. adenophyllus and M. glaber

How to recognise it

  • An African dipterocarp, occurring primarily in the Zambezian region
  • Just one species, Monotes madagascariensis, is known to grow in Madagascar

Beilschmiedia

No common name for the whole genus

Beilschmiedia is a large genus (267 accepted species) of trees and shrubs belonging to the laurel family. Most grow in the tropics; a few grow in cooler regions. They are found all over tropical Asia, Africa, Madagascar, Australia, New Zealand and North, Central and South America, including the Caribbean. They are more tolerant of a variety of climates than most other laurels. Many are found in cloud forest and tropical rainforest, most of them in Asia and Southeast Asia, some in drier areas such as the Mediterranean. Some of them, like the Chilean bellotos B. berteroana and B. miersii, are even frost hardy and are commonly planted in the colder countries.

The leaves are dark green, coriaceous, and have sunken veins. The new leaves emerge red and the young twigs are clothed in fine reddish-brown hairs. The bark can be smooth or rough, and is brown. The flowers are small and greenish to cream and grow in upright clusters. The fruits contain one seed each and vary in size from very large green balls to dark purple, plum-like fruits covered with a waxy film. Many of them are eaten by birds, which spread the seeds. In New Zealand, the taraire's seeds are too large to be swallowed and carried by any birds except the native pigeon. Valuable timber is provided by some species, and the seeds of B. bancroftii were an Aboriginal food.

Source: Wikipedia

Family
Lauraceae
Common names
None for the genus; taraire and tawa (New Zealand) and belloto (Chile) are names of single species
Size
267 accepted species
Origin and range
Tropical Asia, Africa, Madagascar, Australia, New Zealand, North, Central and South America and the Caribbean
Type species
B. roxburghiana (Himalayas to southern China, Indochina and Peninsular Malaysia)
Uses
Valuable timber; seeds of B. bancroftii eaten by Aboriginal Australians; frost-hardy species grown in gardens
Species on this site
B. berteroana and B. costaricensis

How to recognise it

  • Dark green, coriaceous leaves with sunken veins, in pairs or one at a time depending on the species
  • New leaves and young twigs are reddish and covered with fine reddish-brown hairs.
  • Small greenish to cream flowers in upright clusters at the junction of leaves and stem
  • From large green balls to waxy dark purple plums, one-seeded fruits.

Cryptocarya

No common name

About 360 species of Cryptocarya belong to the laurel family. They are mostly trees and a few shrubs, and are found in various parts of South America, southern Africa, Madagascar, south and south-east Asia, Japan, New Guinea and Australia. The genus was first described by the botanist Robert Brown, in 1810. Its name, meaning 'hidden nut', comes from the fact that the fruit is enclosed within parts of the flower. DNA analysis has identified its closest relatives to be Beilschmiedia, Potameia and Endiandra.

The leaves usually grow one at a time along the branches on short stalks, with veins branching out from a central rib. Small flowers are grouped at the tips of the branches. Each has 6 petal-like parts (called tepals) and 9 stamens arranged in two rows, and contains both male and female parts. The fruit is fleshy, oval to round in shape and contains a single seed. There are some species which are also beneficial to humans. Ravensara oil from C. agathophylla from eastern Madagascar is marketed for its use in aromatherapy in Europe and America, while leaves of C. woodii found at prehistoric sites in Africa were probably used as an insect repellent.

Source: Wikipedia

Quick facts

Family
Lauraceae
Size
About 360 species
Origin and range
South America, southern Africa, Madagascar, South and Southeast Asia, Japan, New Guinea and Australia
Name meaning
"Hidden nut", as the fruit is hidden by the tepals
First described
Robert Brown, 1810
Closest relatives
Beilschmiedia, Potameia and Endiandra
Uses
Ravensara oil for aromatherapy (C. agathophylla); insect control in the past (C. woodii)
Species on this site
C. adpressa and C. alba

How to recognise it

  • Leaves typically borne singly on short stalks, with veins branching from a midrib
  • Flowers small; tepals 6 petal-like; stamens 9 in 2 series; clustered near the tips of the branches.
  • Fleshy, oval to round fruit, containing only one seed, concealed by the tepals when young.
