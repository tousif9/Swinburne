Plant Species

Protecting Species

On a global list, known as the IUCN Red List, scientists monitor the threat status of each species. Some of the plants on this page are under threat from logging and farming. Each image you submit using our contribution form helps highlight where they are still found.

About This Page

A species is one particular kind of plant. It has a two-part scientific name with genus first followed by a species name which may describe the plant or location of its occurrence. Now you can discover two species of each of our four genera, with photographs, where each comes from and how to identify it.

How to Identify a Species in the Field

  1. Start by working out the family and genus, using the Families and Genera pages.
  2. Note the leaves: size, shape and hairiness.
  3. See if there are any flowers or fruit to be found. They are the surest of clues.
  4. Record its location since many species are found in only one area.
  5. Upload your photo on the Identify page to check your answer.

Dipterocarpus alatus

Resin tree

The resin tree Dipterocarpus alatus is a large tropical tree, 30 to 60 metres tall. It occurs, sometimes in groups, in tropical Asia in thick evergreen and semi-evergreen lowland forest and along river-banks. It prefers moist soil with good drainage and will withstand flooding, but not strong winds or fire. Its flowers are pollinated by insects and its fruit matures from March through April and the winged seeds are dispersed by the wind.

People have relied on this tree for a long time. Its wood is used for timber, and its resin is extracted for lacquer, boat sealing and traditional medicine. Mixed with beeswax, the resin is also used to dress ulcers. Young trees' bark is used as a traditional remedy for liver disease and rheumatism. The tree is now listed as Vulnerable, and is one of the primary trees being planted to restore forest cover around the Dong Nai River and Cat Tien National Park in Vietnam.

Source: Wikipedia

Dipterocarpus tuberculatus

Gurjun tree (Khmer: khlông)

Dipterocarpus tuberculatus is called the gurjun tree in India and khlông in Cambodia. The plant is a dipterocarp, native to Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos and Vietnam. It grows in open lowland forest and is 5 - 25 metres tall, preferring an altitude of up to about 800 - 1000 metres above sea level.

Numerous uses are made of this tree. It is used for its oil and wood in India and neighbouring countries. In Cambodia, the leaves serve to wrap goods, and the weatherproof wood is used for beams, boards and construction of boats; the roots are also utilized in traditional medicines to assist the healing of broken bones. It is also a source of firewood in some areas of Kampong Chhnang province.

Source: Wikipedia

Monotes adenophyllus

No common name

Monotes adenophyllus is a dipterocarp species found in south-central Africa and is a shrub or small tree. It is found in western and south-western Tanzania, northern Zambia, the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo and northern Angola. Most grow to around 4-8 metres tall, with a few reaching 12 metres.

Search for it in miombo woodland and in bushy and/or wooded savanna, usually at 840-1500 m, on shallow, rocky or iron rich soils (also called lateritic soil). Three forms are recognised by botanists. Var. adenophyllus is widely distributed throughout the range, var. homblei is from Tanzania, northern Zambia, the southern DR Congo and northern Angola, and f. albidus is only known from the southern DR Congo.

Source: Wikipedia

Monotes glaber

No common name

Monotes glaber is a dipterocarp species of south-central Africa. It occurs from the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo through Angola, Zambia and Zimbabwe to northern Botswana. A small tree, 3-10 m tall, which is common in miombo woodland on sandy soil. It was described by botanist Thomas Archibald Sprague in 1909. Its name glaber in Latin means "smooth" or "hairless".

Source: Wikipedia

Beilschmiedia berteroana

Southern acorn tree (Spanish: belloto del sur)

Beilschmiedia berteroana (southern acorn tree or belloto del sur) is a threatened evergreen tree which grows naturally only in Chile, around 35-37°S. It can reach heights of 30 metres, with a trunk up to a metre wide and grey bark which is cut by long vertical fissures. Its glossy green, oval leaves are 3 to 7.5 cm long, grow in pairs or nearly in pairs, and are bluish-grey underneath. In July and August it bears clusters of greenish-yellow flowers. A single seed is in a green fruit 1.5-2 cm in diameter, tipped with a small point.

This tree is able to grow at elevations as high as 1800 metres above sea level, and is more frost tolerant than most of its tropical relatives. It can be seen in Chile in the La Campana National Park where it grows alongside the endangered Chilean wine palm. Its sweet fruit is eaten by local people; its bark was formerly used to tan hides and its handsome tough wood is very useful. Its flowers are the source of honey for bees and it is planted as a garden tree in Chile and occasionally Spain.

Source: Wikipedia

Beilschmiedia costaricensis

No common name

Beilschmiedia costaricensis is an evergreen tree that belongs to the laurel family and is named after Costa Rica. It lives in the highland forests of Central America where it is misty and humid, and where it rains often and it is cooler than in the lowlands. It is typically a medium to large tree that grows to the upper level of the forest.

It has simple leaves, one per node. These are thick and leathery and aid the tree in retaining water. The flowers are small and plain and insects are the primary agents for transport of pollen between flowers. One seed is contained in each fruit. The fruit is eaten by birds and mammals and seed is dropped elsewhere to start young trees. Bark is smooth on young trees, rough and cracked on older trees. Few publications exist on the use of this tree and it is not commonly grown elsewhere than in its home forests.

Source: ePlant AI

Cryptocarya adpressa

No common name (described in 2016)

Cryptocarya adpressa is a species of evergreen tree in the laurel family. It grows only on New Caledonia, a group of islands in the south-west Pacific, and is a fairly new discovery: the botanists Jérôme Munzinger and Gordon McPherson first described it officially in 2016. It grows in dense, humid tropical forest and can reach a height of 6-25 metres. It has a trunk of 15 to 20 centimetres in diameter, and the bark is brown, slightly rough to nearly smooth.

Its leaves are small, with feather-like veins radiating from a central vein. Its distinguishing feature, compared with its relatives Cryptocarya leptospermoides and Cryptocarya gracilis, is the indumentum of fine hairs on the clusters of flowers. These hairs remain in place and pressed against the surface, hence the name – adpressa, which is Latin for "pressed close".

Source: Plants of the World Online (Kew)

Cryptocarya alba

Peumo, Chilean acorn

Cryptocarya alba, known as peumo or the Chilean acorn, is an evergreen tree in central Chile. It is happy in wet or dry places, grows as high as 1500 metres above sea level, and lives with the endangered Chilean wine palm. It can grow to 20 metres in height, having a straight trunk up to a metre wide with greyish bark. The branches are thick and growing from the centre of the tree, with thin twigs hanging at the ends.

Its egg-shaped leaves are between 2.5 and 8.5 centimetres long, wavy and have a strong scent. Its small yellowish-green flowers appear in clusters from November to January and its red berries (also known as peumos) are edible. The fruits are fairly large and the seeds germinate readily. The hard wood withstands wetness, and the bark is used for tanning and to produce an orange dye. Peumo has also been used for ornamental purposes and as a source of fruit and has performed well when planted in northern California and in Spain.

Source: Wikipedia
